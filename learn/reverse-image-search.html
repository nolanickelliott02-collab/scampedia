Reverse Image Search

Last reviewed: Aug 8, 2026 · Free

Reverse image search shows you every other place a photo has shown up online — which reveals whether it's an old stock photo, a picture of someone else being used without their knowledge, or something that's been circulating for years before it landed in your message. It's free, takes under a minute, and works from your phone or a computer.

What This Actually Tells You

A reverse image search doesn't tell you whether a photo is real or fake. What it tells you is where else this exact photo has been — and that's usually more useful.

If someone messages you a photo of a person claiming to be a soldier stationed overseas, a photo can't tell you by looking at it whether that's true. But a reverse image search can tell you that the same photo has been posted on a stock photography site since 2019, or that it's actually a photo of a completely different, named person's public profile. That's not a guess about pixels. That's a fact about where the image has already appeared.

This is why it's worth learning before any of the harder-to-explain AI-detection skills: it doesn't require judging anything about how the image looks. It just shows you its history.

On Your iPhone or iPad

Starting from a Google Images result

  1. Type what you're looking for into Google Images, the same as any search, and open the photo you want to check.
  2. Press and hold your finger on the photo. A menu will pop up — tap "Search image with Google Lens."

Starting from a photo inside a message, post, or website

  1. This works on a photo wherever you find it — a webpage, a social media post, anywhere. Open it in the Google app or Chrome. (Only have Safari? Skip to the next method below — it covers the same ground a different way.)
  2. Press and hold your finger on the photo until a menu pops up.
  3. Tap the search option in that menu — worded "Search inside image" in the Google app, "Search image with Google" in Chrome.
  4. Scroll down to see what turns up. There's usually also a way to add a word or two if you want to narrow it down.

Starting from a photo already saved on your phone

  1. This one works in Safari, too. Open the Google app, or open Chrome or Safari and go to google.com.
  2. Tap the small camera icon inside the search box — that's Google Lens.
  3. Choose the photo from your camera roll instead of taking a new one.
  4. Scroll down to see what comes up.

On a Computer

Upload the file directly

  1. Go to google.com and click "Search by image," then "Upload a file."
  2. Pick the photo from your computer and open it.

Drag it in

  1. Find the image file wherever it's saved on your computer.
  2. Drag it straight into the Google search box — no menus needed.

Search using the photo's web address

  1. If the photo is already on a webpage, right-click it and choose "Copy image address."
  2. Go to google.com, click "Search by image," and paste that address in.
  3. Run the search.

The one-click version, if you use Chrome

  1. Right-click the image directly on the page you found it on.
  2. Choose "Search with Google Lens" and the results open in a panel beside the page — no need to leave it.

What to Look For in the Results

Once you have results, you're looking for a mismatch between what you were told and what you find:

  • The photo is much older than the story. You were told this is a photo from last week; the search shows it's been online for years.
  • The photo shows up attached to a different name. You were told this is "Sarah from the company," but the same photo appears on a modeling site, a stock photo site, or someone else's social media under a different name entirely.
  • The photo appears on many unrelated sites. A single, ordinary personal photo usually doesn't appear across dozens of sites. Wide reuse is a sign it was pulled from somewhere public, not taken by the person messaging you.
  • You find nothing at all. This is genuinely ambiguous — see below.

When It Won't Help

Reverse image search only works if the image has appeared somewhere else before, in a place a search engine has indexed. A photo that's brand new — never posted anywhere, or freshly generated by an AI tool — can come back with no matches at all. No matches doesn't mean the photo is genuine. It just means this particular check has nothing to compare against.

That's not a flaw in the tool, it's a limit worth knowing about honestly: treat "nothing found" as inconclusive, not as reassurance. If a photo turns up no history and something about the situation still feels off — someone you've never met asking for money, an urgent story that doesn't quite add up — the next lesson in this course, on what a message is actually asking you to do, matters more than any image check ever will.
