What to Do When You're Not Sure

Last reviewed: Aug 8, 2026 · Free

When you can't tell whether something's real, the fix isn't to look closer — it's to reach out a different way than the message reached you. Call the number already saved in your phone, not the one you were just given.

The One Habit That Covers Almost Everything

You don't need to become good at spotting fakes. You need one habit: when something asks you to act immediately — send money, share a code, click a link, decide before you've had time to think — stop, and reach that person or organization again through a way you already had, not the way they just reached you.

If your grandson calls saying he's in trouble and needs money wired immediately, hang up and call him back on the number already in your phone. If a text says your bank account is locked, don't tap the link — open your banking app the way you always do, or call the number on the back of your card. If an email says you need to act on a delivery, a bill, or an account today, go to the company's website by typing it in yourself, the way you would any other day.

Almost every scam wants one thing above the money: for you to act inside the conversation it started, using only the contact information it gave you. Stepping outside that conversation is the single most useful thing you can do, and it works whether or not you can tell anything is wrong yet.

Why This Works Even When You Can't Tell

A scammer can fake a voice, a name, a logo, an official-looking message. What they can't fake is the phone number already saved in your contacts, or the web address you type in from memory instead of clicking. The moment you leave the channel they set up and reach out on one you control, you're no longer talking to whoever wrote the message — you're talking to whoever actually answers at the real number.

This is why it matters more than being able to tell a real photo from a fake one, or a real voice from a cloned one. Those are judgment calls, made under pressure, about something designed to fool that exact judgment — and no one gets them right every time. Calling a known number isn't a guarantee either, but it replaces a judgment call with something closer to a fact: either the person you know picks up and says there's no emergency, or the actual bank says there's nothing wrong with your account.

If You Don't Already Have a Number

Sometimes the message claims to be from an organization you don't have saved anywhere — a government agency, a delivery company, a business you've never called before. The same idea still applies: don't use any phone number, email address, or link the message gives you. Look the organization up yourself — a search you do, a bill you already have, a card in your wallet — and contact them through what you find that way.

If you can't find an independent way to reach them at all, that's information too. A real organization that needs something from you is almost always reachable through more than one route.

Slowing Down Is Not Rude

The pressure to answer immediately is doing work for whoever sent the message, not for you. A real emergency involving someone you love will still be real in the ten minutes it takes to call them back directly. A real bill, a real account problem, a real legal notice — none of it becomes worse because you verified it first through a channel you trust. Nobody legitimate is offended by a callback.

If you take one thing from this course, take this: you don't have to get better at spotting what's fake. You just have to get in the habit of checking somewhere else before you act on it — and that habit works on everything, including the scams that haven't been invented yet.
