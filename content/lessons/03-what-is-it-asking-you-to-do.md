## Four Shapes, One System

Most advice about spotting a scam focuses on the story — is the emergency real, is the photo real, is the voice real. Those questions can take a long time to answer, and sometimes can't be answered at all. There's a faster question: not "is this true," but "what is this actually asking me to do, and how is it asking me to do it."

Almost every scam request is built from the same four pieces, and they're not four separate things to individually check off — they work together, each one doing a specific job:

- **Urgency** — "before it's too late," "in the next hour," "today only." Its job is to stop you from taking the time to think or check.
- **Secrecy** — "don't tell anyone," "this needs to stay between us." Its job is to stop you from getting a second opinion from someone who'd spot it instantly.
- **Isolation** — steering you away from a specific person who'd normally help: "don't call your bank," "don't tell your husband," "your daughter can't know yet." Same job as secrecy, aimed at a specific relationship instead of everyone in general.
- **An unusual payment method** — gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, cash in the mail. Its job is to make the money impossible to get back once it's gone.

Take any one of these away and the scam usually falls apart. Take away urgency, and you have time to think. Take away secrecy or isolation, and you tell someone who says "wait, that's not how this works." Take away the unusual payment method, and even if you're fooled, the money can often be recovered. That's why real requests — from real people, real banks, real government agencies — almost never combine more than one of these at a time, and scam requests almost always combine several.

## Why a Real Photo Doesn't Change Any of This

A photo can be completely real — not AI-generated, not stolen from someone else, exactly what it claims to be — and the request attached to it can still be a scam. The photo answers a different question than the one that matters here. It might tell you the picture wasn't faked. It says nothing about whether you're being rushed, isolated, or asked to pay in a way you can't undo.

This is worth sitting with, because it cuts against the instinct this whole course exists to correct: evidence that the *content* is real doesn't tell you anything about whether the *request* is legitimate. A real photo of a real grandchild, sent by someone who has genuinely stolen their identity, attached to an urgent, secretive, wire-transfer request, is still exactly as much a scam as if the photo had been fake. Check the request, not just the evidence around it.

## Recognizing This Is the Whole Job Here

This lesson stops at recognition on purpose. Once you notice urgency, secrecy, isolation, or an unusual payment method — especially more than one at once — [What to Do When You're Not Sure](what-to-do-when-not-sure.html) covers what to actually do about it. The two matter in different ways: this is about learning to see the shape quickly, before you're already halfway into it. That one is about the one habit that gets you out.
