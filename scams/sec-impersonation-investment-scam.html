SEC Impersonation Investment Scam

Added Nov 24, 2026· 🤖 AI-researched· Editor: Nick Elliott· Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Investor.gov — "SEC Impersonators May Lure Investors Into Scams Through Social Media or Text Messages -- Investor Alert"

Overview

Scammers pose as the SEC or its officials on social media and in text messages to pitch stock tips, demand advance fees, or promise to help investors recover lost money — all designed to steal funds or account credentials.

How It Works

According to a September 2025 SEC Investor Alert, fraudsters impersonate the SEC itself or real SEC officials and employees on social media or in text messages in order to solicit people for scams, including fake stock tips, advance fee fraud, and offers to help victims get their money back. These fake posts and messages often use the SEC's official seal, a link to the agency's real website, or the actual name or photo of a real SEC official to appear legitimate; one documented example was a false social media profile impersonating a sitting SEC Commissioner. Victims may also receive unsolicited texts falsely claiming to be from the SEC, even though the agency does not recommend investment groups, officially or unofficially. Separately, the SEC has also warned about impersonators who misrepresent that they work for the agency and pretend to help investors confirm trades or purchase stock, reading back trade details to sound official, while actually just stealing the victim's money. Communications claiming to be from the SEC can arrive by phone call, voicemail, text, social media message, email, letter, or fake certificate, and scammers may also target public company officers or employees of broker-dealers and investment advisers by posing as senior SEC staff, or direct investors to imposter websites by posting comments on the real social media accounts of brokers and advisers.

Red Flags

  • Unsolicited contact claiming to be from the SEC via text message, social media DM, or phone call
  • A request for your shareholdings, account numbers, PINs, passwords, or other account-access information
  • Correspondence claiming to confirm a specific securities transaction or trade on the SEC's behalf
  • Use of the SEC seal, logo, or a real official's name/photo in a social media post or message promoting a stock tip or investment group
  • An offer to help you recover money you already lost to a scam in exchange for a fee or personal information

Real Examples

  • A documented false social media profile impersonated sitting SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce to lend credibility to a scam post
  • Investors received unsolicited text messages falsely claiming to be from the SEC, in one case promoting an investment group despite the SEC stating it never recommends such groups
  • An earlier SEC alert documented an actual phone call transcript of a fraudster posing as an SEC employee telling an investor he would 'record the details of the trade' to sound official before stealing the person's money
  • Fraudsters have posted comments impersonating SEC staff on the real social media accounts of brokers and investment advisers to redirect victims to imposter websites

How to Protect Yourself

  • Only engage with the SEC through its verified social media accounts listed at sec.gov/opa/socialmedia
  • If you're unsure whether a message is really from the SEC, call the SEC's Office of Investor Education and Assistance directly at (800) 732-0330 or email Help@SEC.gov before responding
  • Remember the SEC does not contact investors to confirm trades, set up trading accounts, or endorse any specific securities, products, or individuals
  • Never share account numbers, PINs, passwords, or shareholding details with anyone contacting you out of the blue claiming to be a regulator
  • Report any suspicious communication claiming to be from the SEC using the Investor Complaint Form on investor.gov or to the SEC's Office of Inspector General
