Fake Concert & Event Ticket Resale Scam

Added Nov 7, 2026· 🤖 AI-researched· Editor: Nick Elliott· Source: Better Business Bureau / WLS-AM, Guardio

Overview

Scammers build convincing fake ticket-resale websites and social media listings — now often using AI to generate realistic PDFs with fake barcodes — to sell tickets to concerts, festivals and sporting events that don't exist or that get resold to multiple buyers.

How It Works

As in-demand concerts, festivals and sporting events sell out, scammers create lookalike ticketing websites, fake "verified resale" listings, phony waitlist pages, and social media posts (especially on Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist) offering tickets at tempting prices. Buyers pay by card, Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, wire transfer, or crypto and either receive nothing, a PDF or screenshot "ticket" that was already sold to several other people, or a counterfeit electronic ticket/wristband that fails to scan at the gate. Increasingly, AI tools are used to generate convincing fake ticket PDFs complete with realistic barcodes, venue branding and seat details, or to clone a legitimate resale platform's login page to steal both payment and account credentials.

Red Flags

  • Price for a sold-out event is at or below face value
  • Seller wants payment via Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, wire, gift cards, or crypto instead of a buyer-protected method
  • Seller refuses to transfer the ticket through the official platform app (Ticketmaster, etc.) and only offers a PDF, screenshot, or photo of a barcode
  • Listing found on Facebook Marketplace or Craigslist rather than an official or verified resale channel
  • High-pressure urgency such as 'other buyers are interested' or a fake countdown/waitlist page
  • Website looks like an official ticketing platform but has a slightly altered URL or recently created domain

Real Examples

  • The BBB warned Lollapalooza fans in July 2026 about fake ticket websites, phony 'Wait List' pages, and counterfeit wristbands designed to steal money and personal information.
  • BBB data cited via Surfshark Research found that 38% of ticket scam cases in 2025 involved fake websites built to look like official resale platforms, with Facebook accounting for another 28% of cases.
  • A seller sends what looks like a real e-ticket, but the same barcode has already been sold to other buyers or the seller keeps a copy and uses it first, so the ticket shows as already used at the door — a pattern seen with PDFs and screenshots sent instead of an official in-app transfer.

How to Protect Yourself

  • Buy only through the event's official ticketing partner or a platform's verified resale option (e.g., Ticketmaster Verified Resale)
  • Confirm the seller can transfer the ticket digitally through the official app in front of you, since a PDF or screenshot proves nothing and real transfers invalidate the seller's copy
  • Pay with a credit card or buyer-protected service so you can dispute the charge if the ticket is fake
  • Avoid buying tickets through social media marketplaces or classifieds, which are hotspots for counterfeit listings
  • Be skeptical of prices at or below face value for a sold-out show and of pressure to decide quickly
