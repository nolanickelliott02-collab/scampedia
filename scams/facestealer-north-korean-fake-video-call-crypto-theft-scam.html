"FaceStealer" North Korean Fake Video Call Crypto Theft Scam

Added Nov 14, 2026· 🤖 AI-researched· Editor: Nick Elliott· Source: FBI/IC3 FLASH AC-111226-MS, reported by The Hacker News:

Overview

North Korean hackers hijack a victim's messaging accounts, then use stolen webcam recordings (and sometimes AI deepfakes) of that victim to lure their contacts into fake video-conferencing calls that trick them into installing crypto-draining malware.

How It Works

The FBI describes a six-stage, self-repeating chain called FaceStealer. First, hackers take over a victim's accounts on platforms like Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack or Discord and scan them for crypto wallets, seed phrases and contact lists while secretly recording the victim's webcam. They then use a legitimate scheduling tool like Calendly to invite one of that victim's real contacts to a "meeting," but the link actually opens a fake video-conferencing site built to look like Zoom, Microsoft Teams or Google Meet. When the new target joins, the attackers play the stolen webcam footage of the person they know (sometimes supplemented with an AI-generated deepfake of a well-known crypto or Web3 figure) to simulate a normal, trusted call, occasionally with extra fake "participants" for social proof. Partway through, the attacker claims a technical glitch, saying the microphone isn't working, and sends a "fix" link or script to resolve it. Running that fix installs malware that captures keystrokes, takes screenshots, harvests browser cookies and opens a backdoor, while also quietly recording the new victim's own webcam from the moment they clicked the link. With that access, the hackers drain cryptocurrency, hijack the victim's messaging accounts, and repeat the entire scheme against that person's own contacts, letting each victim unknowingly recruit the next.

Red Flags

  • An unexpected video meeting invite, even one that appears to come from a real contact's account
  • A scheduling link (e.g., Calendly) that redirects to an unfamiliar or oddly-branded video conferencing website instead of the official Zoom/Teams/Meet app
  • A call where the other person's video feels delayed, scripted, or slightly off, or extra silent 'participants' are present
  • The host or a participant says their microphone isn't working and asks you to click a link or run a script/update to fix it
  • Pressure to quickly download software or enter a code during the call to join or fix a technical issue

Real Examples

  • The FBI said the FaceStealer scheme caused more than $400 million in cryptocurrency theft over the last 18 months, and more than 900 victim webcam video recordings used by the threat actors to impersonate victims in follow-on attacks were seized by the FBI
  • The scheme was used to target the maintainer of a popular open-source JavaScript (npm) package, the axios package, to push a trojanized version of the package that delivered a cross-platform remote access trojan
  • Hackers hijacked accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord and used them to send meeting invitations to the victim's contacts, impersonating the victim to propose a Zoom or Teams meeting under the pretext of discussing an investment opportunity

Where It Spreads

Video Conferencing Apps (fake Zoom/Teams/Google Meet)TelegramLinkedInSlackDiscord

How to Protect Yourself

  • Verify any unexpected meeting invitation through a separate channel (call or text the person directly) before joining, even if it looks like it's from someone you know
  • Never download software, browser extensions, or run scripts during a video call to fix an audio or video problem
  • Keep your camera and microphone off until you've confirmed the host's identity live
  • Only join meetings through official apps using an official invite code rather than clicking embedded links
