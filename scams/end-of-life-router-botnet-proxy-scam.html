End-of-Life Router Botnet Proxy Scam

Added Nov 9, 2026· 🤖 AI-researched· 🏛️ Government-Verified· Editor: Nick Elliott· Source: FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), "Cyber Criminal Proxy Services Exploiting End of Life Routers"

Overview

Cybercriminals are hacking old, unsupported ("end of life") home and small-business routers and turning them into hidden proxy networks used to anonymize crimes like cryptocurrency theft and other illegal online activity.

How It Works

The FBI warns that when a router reaches "end of life," the manufacturer stops selling it and stops releasing software updates or security patches, leaving it exposed to known vulnerabilities that never get fixed. Criminal actors exploit these outdated devices (especially older models with remote administration left turned on) and infect them using variants of a botnet malware called TheMoon, which was first discovered on compromised routers back in 2014. Once compromised, the router is secretly turned into a proxy, meaning when the criminals use it to access a website to steal cryptocurrency or contract other illegal services, the site sees the hacked router's IP address instead of the real criminal's location, letting them operate anonymously while the router owner is left unaware their home network is being used as cover for crime.

Red Flags

  • A home or office router is several years old (the FBI flags routers dated 2010 or earlier as high risk) and no longer receives firmware updates
  • Remote administration is enabled on the router without the owner's knowledge or intentional setup
  • The router shows signs of infection such as overheating, connectivity problems, or settings changes the administrator does not recognize
  • The router brand or model is no longer supported or sold by the manufacturer

Real Examples

  • The FBI describes end-of-life routers being breached by cyber actors using variants of TheMoon malware botnet, which installs proxies on victim routers to let cyber actors conduct cyber crimes anonymously, such as stealing cryptocurrency or contracting illegal services.

Where It Spreads

Home RoutersSmall Business NetworksInternet of Things (IoT) Devices

How to Protect Yourself

  • Check whether your router is still supported by the manufacturer and replace it with an updated model if it has reached end of life
  • Disable remote administration features on your router unless you specifically need and understand them
  • Immediately apply any available security patches and firmware updates for your devices
  • Use strong, unique, random passwords and avoid reusing passwords or using password hints
  • Report suspected compromise or related cybercrime to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at www.ic3.gov
