Phone Return Scam (Fake Mobile Upgrade / Redirected Device)

Added Nov 15, 2026· 🤖 AI-researched· Editor: Nick Elliott· Source: Malwarebytes

Overview

Scammers call pretending to be your mobile carrier offering a free phone upgrade, then after a real device is delivered to your home, trick you into "returning" it using a fake shipping label that sends it straight to the criminals — leaving you stuck with the bill.

How It Works

The scam usually starts with a call from a spoofed number impersonating a mobile provider, offering a free phone upgrade, loyalty reward, or unusually attractive deal. After collecting personal information needed for a "credit check," the scammer orders a real new phone in the victim's name using the victim's actual account, and a genuine device is delivered to the victim's home, which makes the offer feel completely legitimate. The caller then says the wrong device was sent, or that an extra phone arrived by mistake, and instructs the victim to send it back using a return label or shipping QR code the caller supplies. Instead of routing the package to the provider's actual returns facility, that label sends the device to an address chosen by the criminals, so the victim ends up with neither the phone nor proof of return, while the provider still shows the device as unreturned and bills the victim for it or locks them into a new contract.

Red Flags

  • An unexpected call offering a free phone, loyalty reward, or unusually attractive upgrade
  • Caller asks for personal information or account credentials to run a supposed credit check before sending a device
  • Instructions to use a return label or shipping QR code supplied directly by the caller rather than the carrier
  • A return destination or shipping process you have not independently confirmed with your provider
  • Pressure to act quickly on a 'wrong device sent by mistake' story

Real Examples

  • Malwarebytes' Scam Guard threat research identified anonymized US records in which callers impersonated Canadian mobile provider Rogers, sent victims a real phone, then used a fake return label to redirect the device to a criminal-controlled address
  • Security researchers described the pattern as sending a real device to build trust, then diverting the 'return' shipment through a prepaid label or QR code that bypassed the carrier's actual returns facility, leaving customers billed for a phone they no longer had

How to Protect Yourself

  • Never use a return label, QR code, or shipping address given to you over the phone — get return instructions only through your provider's official app, website, or a number you look up yourself
  • Don't share account passwords, PINs, or verification codes with anyone who calls you unexpectedly, even if they sound like your carrier
  • If a device you didn't order arrives, contact your provider directly through verified contact channels before doing anything with it
  • If you've already shipped a phone using a caller-provided label, contact your provider immediately, flag the order for a fraud investigation, and reset your account password/PIN
